The question

Why was Brazil so unequal, and what does the nineteenth century tell us?

Brazil has long ranked among the most unequal societies on record. Scholars disagree about when that pattern took hold. Some trace it to the colonial world of plantations and slavery, others to the export boom of the late nineteenth century, and a recent reconstruction to a rise that began only around 1900.

The nineteenth century sits at the center of that dispute, and it is the period we know least about. Income per person roughly doubled between 1820 and 1900, yet how that income was shared among free people remains unclear. The project reads the National Guard’s registers to see that distribution from the inside, town by town, in the decades when the imperial state took shape.

The sources

A militia that measured its citizens

Created in 1831, the National Guard enrolled every free or freed man aged 18 to 60 whose yearly income met a legal minimum, about 700,000 men by the 1860s. Each year a council of local officers met in every parish, heard each man’s age, marital status, occupation, and income, and wrote down what it accepted as true. After 1850 those officers were appointed, so the men who judged a neighbor’s income could also call him to duty or excuse him from it.

The registers therefore record a political judgment as much as a measured income. Enslaved people, women, foreigners, and the poorest free men do not appear on them.

The argument

Inequality made into citizenship

The National Guard turned the census of citizenship into a means of ranking free men. Its councils wrote most men at the minimum and gave rank to wealthier men, so the registers record a hierarchy of citizenship more than a full distribution of income. Low measured inequality in nineteenth-century Brazil owes much to how such records were made.

The floor is a category

Organizations that sort people into categories reward everyone in a category alike. A council that wrote two men in three at 200$000 was sorting them into a single category.

Property tells another story

Estate inventories show wealth far more concentrated than the registers suggest, in Rio de Janeiro, in Batatais, and across São Paulo’s towns.

Colonial tools, postcolonial timing

The steepest hierarchies appear on coffee frontiers opened in the nineteenth century, built with enslaved labor and land claimed while the state stood aside.

The state as installer

In September 1850 the imperial parliament ended the Atlantic slave trade, made purchase the only lawful route to public land, and placed the Guard under appointed officers.

On this reading, the nineteenth century gave Brazil’s inequality a bureaucratic and civic form, a citizenship graded by income and administered by the men at its top. That form outlived the Guard.

Next phase

From fifty registers to a national series

The pilot rests on registers transcribed by hand. The next phase aims to extend the series across the Empire and open it to other scholars.
